# Tipping in China

Traveling to a new country is always exciting, but it also means navigating unfamiliar customs. Whether it’s language, currency, or social norms, having some cultural context helps ensure a respectful and responsible trip.

One question we often receive is: “Do I need to tip in China?” While tipping is standard practice in the U.S. and parts of Europe, both as a gesture of appreciation and as a vital part of service workers’ income, in China, tipping is not a common practice.

So, what does that mean for international travelers visiting China? Here’s a simple breakdown of when tipping is, and isn’t, appropriate.

 electricity Tipping Culture? There isn’t one – Traditionally

Recently, cultural differences like tipping have sparked discussion on Chinese social media platforms like Xiaohongshu (RedNote), especially after an influx of international users joined the platform. Many foreigners asked: Is it rude to tip in China? Do Chinese people tip at all?

While responses from Chinese users varied, most agree that tipping simply isn’t a part of traditional Chinese culture.

Part of the reason tipping hasn’t taken root is that there’s neither infrastructure nor social expectation for it. China’s digital economy, which is dominated by Alipay and WeChat Pay, runs on fixed payment QR codes, leaving little room for spontaneous gratuities. On food delivery or ride-hailing apps like Didi, Meituan, or Eleme, there’s no built-in tipping system like the percentage-based prompts found in their Western counterparts. Some apps offer a “red packet” (红包 hóngbāo) feature allowing users to send a small token of thanks, but it’s optional and rarely used except for truly outstanding services.

Tipping isn’t rude, but it’s also not expected in most everyday situations. You don’t tip when taking a taxi, dining at a local restaurant, or grabbing a coffee, for example.

 Tipping in Modern China: Do’s and Don’ts

That said, in major international cities like Beijing, Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Macau, norms are shifting—especially in businesses that cater to international travelers. In these settings, tipping is becoming more accepted, and sometimes even customary.

 electricity Where Tipping Is Appropriate

Tour Guides and Private Drivers

In the international travel industry, tipping tour guides and private drivers is expected and appreciated. At WildChina, we take great care in selecting and training our guides, ensuring they are well-equipped to provide exceptional service. While we ensure our team receives fair compensation, gratuities are a welcomed way to acknowledge excellent service.

It is important to note that tips are optional and should be based on your satisfaction of the service provided.

 Luxury Hotels and Restaurants

If you are eating in regular local restaurants, there is no need to tip. Bills are usually paid at the front counter. Plus, as we outlined above, it is usually difficult to tip with digital payments.

However, at luxury or international hotels, particularly those serving foreign guests, tipping is becoming more common. A small tip of ¥10–¥20 (around $1–$3) for bellboys, housekeeping, or concierge staff is appreciated.

For upscale restaurants, some may include a 10–20% service charge on the bill. Check your receipt before adding anything extra.

 electricity Tipping like a local

In China, giving a red envelope (红包 hóngbāo) is a traditional way to express good will and gratitude. Over Chinese New Year, elders give red envelopes to children as a symbol of good luck. People also give red envelopes at weddings, birthdays, or sometimes in professional settings as a polite and discreet gift.

Because peer-to-peer transactions are not available for international users on Alipay or WeChat Pay (see Navigating China’s Digital Frontier: A Guide to Using Alipay and Weixin Pay in 2025), we recommend carrying some cash for tipping purposes.

If you choose to tip your guide or driver in cash, you can place the money in an envelope provided by your hotel. You can also purchase a packet of red envelopes (they come in all kinds of beautiful designs) at the convenience store or ask your tour guide. Handing the envelope personally and with a smile is a very thoughtful gesture and is bound to be appreciated by your guide or driver. If no envelopes are available, however, handing the guide or driver cash directly is also perfectly acceptable.

 Summary

Tipping in China isn’t expected in most circumstances, but that doesn’t mean it’s unwelcome. When in doubt, observe what locals do, and feel free to ask your guide or hotel staff for advice. Ultimately, the best “tip” you can give is kindness, respect, and genuine appreciation for good service.
